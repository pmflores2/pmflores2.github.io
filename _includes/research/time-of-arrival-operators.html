The big picture

In everyday life, "when did it arrive?" is an easy question. In quantum mechanics it is surprisingly hard. Position, momentum and energy are all observables, things you can represent with an operator and measure, but time is treated as a background parameter and there is no obvious "clock operator". Yet detectors click at definite moments, so the arrival of a quantum particle must be describable somehow.

This project (my PhD work at the University of the Philippines Diliman, with Eric Galapon) builds time-of-arrival (TOA) operators: we take the classical formula for when a particle reaches a point and turn it into a quantum operator, then ask what it predicts. Two questions drove most of the work:

  • How long does quantum tunneling take? A particle can cross a barrier it classically cannot climb. Reported tunneling times range from "finite" to "zero" depending on the method, which has been a long-standing controversy. Our TOA-based analysis finds tunneling to be instantaneous, and explains the seemingly contradictory results by separating partial and full tunneling processes. We also checked whether instantaneity was an artifact of using non-relativistic physics by repeating the analysis for relativistic particles.
  • Do quantum objects fall like classical ones? Galileo's weak equivalence principle says everything falls the same way regardless of mass. Using a TOA operator for a particle in free fall, we found small quantum corrections that depend on mass, i.e. a quantum violation of the principle. A related paper shows how quantum and classical clocks can be synchronized.
What we do (the technical version)
  • Quantization ambiguity. The classical TOA can be quantized in infinitely many ways (Weyl, Born-Jordan, and others). We compare their predictions, e.g. for free fall, where different quantizations give different ℏ-dependent corrections, and for tunneling, where we show the instantaneous result holds within a broader class of operators.
  • Confined and relativistic operators. We study confined TOA operators (compact, self-adjoint, with resolvents and covariance properties we can compute), and the relativistic free-motion TOA operator for massive spin-0 particles, using a rigged Hilbert space extension of Razavi's operator. A modified Weyl-ordering rule gives a quantized relativistic TOA operator used to compute traversal times across a square barrier.
  • Quantum clocks. The quantum correction to the classical arrival time can be removed up to a given order in ℏ by choosing an appropriate position-dependent phase of the wavefunction.
